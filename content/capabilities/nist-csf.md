The NIST Cybersecurity Framework is the most widely used structure in the world for describing a security programme. Published by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology and revised as **CSF 2.0** in 2024, it organises everything an organisation does about cyber risk into six functions — Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover — and under them twenty-two categories and a hundred and six specific outcomes. It is voluntary and it is not certifiable. Its value is as a language: a way for a security team, a board, a customer and a regulator to look at the same picture.

That is why it appears in so many places. Boards use it to ask "where are we?" and get an answer that is neither a vulnerability count nor a reassurance. US customers and investors ask suppliers for a profile against it. Cyber insurers structure their questionnaires on it. Regulators from India to Europe reference it. And security leaders use it to reconcile the overlapping demands of ISO 27001, PCI DSS, RBI, SEBI and IRDAI into one programme with one roadmap.

## Profiles, not scores

The framework's central device is the profile: a description of which outcomes an organisation achieves today (the current profile) and which it needs to achieve (the target profile), with the gap between them as the roadmap. The discipline is in the target. A target of "everything at the highest tier" is not a strategy; it is an unfunded wish. A target set from risk appetite, regulatory obligation and what customers actually require produces a roadmap that spends money where exposure is.

GISPL's CSF engagements produce both profiles with evidence behind every score, a roadmap prioritised by risk reduction, a crosswalk that shows where the same control satisfies ISO, PCI and your regulator, and a one-page posture report the board can read and track. The re-assessment a year later is what turns it from a document into a measurement.
