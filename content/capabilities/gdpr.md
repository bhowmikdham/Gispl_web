faq:
- q: We are in India — why does an EU regulation apply to us?
  a: Article 3 gives the GDPR extraterritorial reach — it applies to any organisation offering goods or services to people in the EU or monitoring their behaviour, and to any processor working for an EU controller. If a European client sends you personal data, you are in scope, and your contract with them will say so.
- q: What is the difference between a controller and a processor?
  a: A controller decides why and how personal data is processed; a processor processes it on the controller's instructions. Most Indian outsourcing and SaaS companies are processors for their European clients, which brings direct obligations under Article 28 and direct liability — but a narrower set than the controller's.
- q: How do we transfer data from Europe to India lawfully?
  a: India has no adequacy decision, so transfers need a mechanism — most often the 2021 Standard Contractual Clauses, backed by a transfer impact assessment of Indian law and supplementary measures such as encryption. We prepare all three.
- q: Does GDPR compliance cover the DPDP Act?
  a: Much of it. The DPDP Act's consent, purpose limitation, rights and breach-notification requirements have close GDPR analogues, though the details differ — notably DPDP's consent-manager framework and its own reporting timelines. We build one programme mapped to both.
- q: Do we need a Data Protection Officer?
  a: Mandatory if you are a public body, if your core activities involve large-scale regular monitoring, or if you process special-category data at scale. Many organisations outside those criteria appoint one anyway, or use a fractional DPO. Non-EU organisations in scope also need an EU representative under Article 27.

The General Data Protection Regulation is the European Union's data-protection law and, because of how it is written, one of the most widely applicable laws in the world. It governs any processing of the personal data of people in the EU and EEA — by organisations inside Europe, and by organisations anywhere else that offer them goods or services, monitor their behaviour, or process data on behalf of a European client. For an Indian company with European customers, or one that serves as a processor for a European enterprise, it applies as directly as any domestic law.

The Regulation rests on seven principles — lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; and accountability — and then makes them concrete: a lawful basis for every processing purpose, records of processing, data-protection impact assessments for high-risk activities, rights for individuals to access, correct, erase and port their data, security appropriate to the risk, breach notification to the regulator within seventy-two hours, and strict conditions on moving data outside Europe.

## Where programmes go wrong

Most GDPR work stops at the privacy notice. The notice is the least of it. The obligations that regulators and clients actually test are structural: can you produce your records of processing; can you show the lawful basis for each purpose; do your processor contracts contain the Article 28 terms; have you assessed the transfers to India; could you notify a breach in seventy-two hours; could you answer an access request in a month. These are systems and process questions, and they need to be answered from the systems.

GISPL builds GDPR programmes from the data map outward. For processors, the engagement centres on Article 28: the contract, the sub-processor chain, the transfer mechanism and the security measures a European client's audit will examine. For controllers, it covers the full accountability framework. And because most of our clients are also subject to India's DPDP Act, we map the two together so the organisation runs one privacy programme rather than two.
