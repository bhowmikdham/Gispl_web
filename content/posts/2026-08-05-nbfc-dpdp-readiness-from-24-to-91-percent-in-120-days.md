*A representative engagement. The client is a non-banking financial company whose identity is withheld under NDA; figures are illustrative and rounded so that no system or team can be identified.*

## The situation

A lending NBFC with about 2.1 million customers had done what most organisations do first: it had a privacy policy, a consent checkbox on the loan-application form, and a data-protection policy signed off by the board. What it did not have was a map of where personal data actually lived.

Customer data sat in a core lending system, a CRM, two collections tools, a marketing platform, a data warehouse, 14 vendor integrations and — the discovery exercise later found — a shared drive of exported spreadsheets that predated the CRM.

With the Consent Manager mandate fixed for 13 November 2026 and full enforcement six months after, the board asked one question: *"If a customer asks us to delete their data tomorrow, can we?"* The honest answer was no.

## What we did

The programme ran the eight DPDP workstreams in order, because each one depends on the one before it.

**Gap assessment (weeks 1–3).** Every personal-data flow was scored against every DPDP obligation. The readiness score at the start was 24%: strong on policy, weak on everything that touches a system.

**Data discovery (weeks 2–7).** Personal data was mapped across 38 systems, cloud estates and endpoints, including the spreadsheet drive nobody had declared. Every store was tagged with purpose, lawful basis, retention and the vendor that could reach it.

**Policy and consent framework (weeks 5–9).** The privacy policy and consent notices were rewritten for the processing the NBFC actually does, purpose by purpose, in plain language. A consent-management framework was designed around the registered Consent Manager integration due in November.

**Consent and technical controls (weeks 8–14).** Consent capture was wired into the loan-origination journey and the marketing platform, versioned against the notice the customer saw. Retention and erasure schedules were enforced in the warehouse and the CRM rather than described in a document.

**Data-principal rights (weeks 10–15).** A request-handling process with statutory clocks, a grievance channel that reaches a named officer, and a tracker that proves the answer went out on time.

**Vendor compliance and breach management (weeks 11–16).** Fourteen processor agreements updated; a breach-notification runbook written to the Act's reporting duty and rehearsed once with the incident-response team.

**Training (weeks 13–16).** Role-based sessions for collections, marketing and engineering — the three teams that handle the most personal data and had the least idea they did.

**Compliance validation (weeks 16–17).** A formal Compliance Readiness Assessment, signed off for the board.

## The numbers

| Workstream | Readiness at start | Readiness at close |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gap assessment | 20% | 100% |
| Data discovery | 15% | 92% |
| Policy development | 35% | 100% |
| Consent & technical controls | 22% | 88% |
| Data-principal rights | 18% | 90% |
| Vendor compliance & breach management | 25% | 85% |
| Training & awareness | 30% | 95% |
| Compliance validation | 10% | 100% |
| **Overall readiness** | **24%** | **91%** |

Time to answer an erasure request went from "unknown, probably weeks" to under six hours, with a record of completeness. The remaining 9% is a vendor migration scheduled for the following quarter, tracked as accepted risk.

## What changed for the NBFC

- **Discovery came first, and it was the hard part.** Nine of the fourteen weeks of engineering effort went into finding and tagging data. Everything after that was faster than the client expected, because the map existed.
- **Consent became a system property.** A customer's consent is now recorded against the exact version of the notice they saw, so the NBFC can prove what was agreed and when.
- **Rights requests became routine.** Access, correction and erasure are handled inside the statutory window with evidence, instead of by an engineer running ad-hoc SQL.
- **The board got a number.** Readiness is reported quarterly as a score with a trend, not as a policy list.

## If this looks like your organisation

Policies are the easy part of DPDP, and most organisations already have them. The obligations that decide whether you can honour a rights request or report a breach on time live in systems, and systems need a data map first. Start with discovery; the deadline does not move.
