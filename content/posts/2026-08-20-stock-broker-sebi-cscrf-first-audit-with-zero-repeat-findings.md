*A representative engagement. The client is a SEBI-regulated stock broker whose identity is withheld under NDA; figures are illustrative and rounded so that no system or team can be identified.*

## The situation

A mid-size stock broker — an exchange member with retail and institutional desks, a trading app and a co-located order-management system — fell into the "mid-size regulated entity" category under SEBI's Cybersecurity and Cyber Resilience Framework. That meant a first audit by 30 June 2026, performed by a CERT-IN empanelled organisation, in SEBI's format.

The broker was ISO 27001 certified and assumed that would carry most of the weight. It carried some. CSCRF is structured around six domains — Governance, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover — and SEBI asks for evidence in each, mapped to its control set, not a certificate that describes intent.

## What we did

**Scope and categorise (week 1).** We confirmed the entity category, the systems in scope, and the exact submission format SEBI expected, in writing, before the audit began. Two systems the broker had assumed were out of scope — a vendor-hosted back-office and the co-location link — were in.

**Audit and evidence (weeks 2–5).** A full-scope assessment against the CSCRF control set by CERT-IN empanelled auditors, opening and closing with board-level entry and exit meetings as the current CERT-IN guidelines require. Where ISO 27001 evidence was reusable it was mapped rather than recreated; where it was not, the gap was written up as an observation with the evidence SEBI would want.

**Remediate with guidance (weeks 4–9).** The 47 observations were ranked by regulatory exposure — what SEBI would query first — rather than by control number. The broker's team closed them with our guidance while the evidence trail was built alongside, so every closure came with the artefact that proved it.

**Report, retest and submit (weeks 9–10).** Closed observations were retested. The submission-ready audit report, the retest results and the evidence index went to the compliance officer three weeks before the 30 June deadline.

## The numbers

| Domain | Observations at audit | Open at submission |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Governance | 6 | 0 |
| Identify | 9 | 0 |
| Protect | 14 | 0 |
| Detect | 8 | 0 |
| Respond | 6 | 0 |
| Recover | 4 | 0 |
| **Total** | **47** | **0** |

Twenty-nine of the 47 observations were closed with evidence the broker already held but had never mapped to a CSCRF control. Eighteen needed real work — most of it in Protect and Detect, where the trading app's logging and the co-location link's monitoring did not meet the standard. None repeated at retest.

## What changed for the broker

- **ISO 27001 helped, and it was not enough.** Roughly 60% of the evidence was reusable. The rest lived in the two domains that regulators care about most and certifications test least: detection and response.
- **Scope was the first surprise.** Two systems the broker had planned to leave out were in scope. Discovering that in week one, rather than in SEBI's queries, was the single biggest time saving of the engagement.
- **The evidence base outlives the audit.** CSCRF is annual — half-yearly for qualified stock brokers. The evidence index built for the first submission is now maintained continuously, so the next cycle is an update rather than a reconstruction.
- **The compliance officer had answers.** When the exchange asked follow-up questions, every one was answered from the evidence index, the same day.
